Do not include PHI

Before any content goes into a portfolio file, note, or AI prompt:

  • Never include patient identifiers — Names, initials, MRNs, dates of birth, room/bed numbers.
  • Watch for identifying combinations — A rare procedure + a date + a unit can point at one specific patient.
  • Use roles instead of names — “my preceptor,” “charge nurse” — never “Jane R. on 4 West.”
  • No screenshots or photos of work — EHR screenshots and printed MARs violate privacy outright.
  • No secrets, ever — Logins, badge numbers, API keys stay out of files and prompts.

Full rules: PHI Safety.

CEU Summaries

Continuing-education units are the rarest currency in a portfolio because they are hard to retell — you remember taking the module, not what it said. A CEU summary is a short, structured record that makes any course, module, or conference session retrievable in one glance, forever.

CEU records are about you, so they are among the safest things in a portfolio — the only PHI risk is a case study inside a course description. Summarize the lesson, never the case.

Educational content only. Whether a course counts toward renewal, how many contact hours a module is worth, and what your state board accepts is decided by your state board of nursing and your certifying body. Log what they say, not what a summary template implies.

The summary card

Five fields, one paragraph, no excuses:

Field Filled example (sanitized)
Title & provider"Sepsis recognition in adults" — online module, ~2 contact hours (per provider)
Date taken2026-03-14
FormatSelf-paced online, with a post-test
3 things I learned(a) red-flag vitals trends beat single readings; (b) newest bundle emphasizes early-liberation practices; (c) when to escalate vs. recheck
How it changed meAdded a trending-assessment habit to my shift routine; gave me language for running the next mock scenario

Keep "3 things I learned" to ≥3 bullet-sized phrases you could explain out loud in an elevator. If you can't produce three, you were attending, not learning — which is also useful information.

The AI loop for CEU summaries

  1. Right after the course, you write 3–5 messy bullet points from the handout/notes — sanitized as always (01-phi-safety.md).
  2. You prompt the AI (see 07-ai-prompt-library.md) to turn bullets into a five-field card and a one-row log entry.
  3. AI returns the structured summary, maybe a tighter "How it changed me."
  4. You verify: hours match the certificate, provider name spelled correctly, date accurate. The certificate is truth, not the AI.

Worked prompt (yours to adapt):

Here are my rough notes from a continuing-education course (no patient info). Turn them into: a 5-field summary card (Title & provider, Date, Format, 3 things I learned, How it changed me) plus a single-row log entry (Date, Title, Provider, Hours, Category, 1-line takeaway). Notes: 2 hr module on sepsis recognition from an online CE provider, taken this week; big idea was unifying trends over single readings, bundle criteria for escalation, and I want to add trend-assessment to my routine.

Categories worth assigning

Use 4–5 that map to what your employer and board actually care about:

  • Clinical (disease, procedures, safety bundles)
  • Professional practice (ethics, delegation, documentation)
  • Leadership/teaching (precepting, charge prep)
  • Technology (EHR skills, informatics)
  • Wellbeing & resilience (stress, fatigue, healthy shift patterns)

Two categories per year usually dominate; both counts and categories read well when your annual review asks "what did you learn this year?" — see 06-annual-review.md.

Anti-patterns

  • Logging only when renewal season forces you. A 5-minute ritual after each course keeps the log real.
  • Copying the course abstract as your takeaway — you should sound like a person who did the training, not a brochure.
  • Inventing hours, saving a screenshot of the certificate and calling it done — file the PDF AND a card; screenshots of certificates belong in your own private records folder, not in a portfolio you share.
  • Putting real case details from a course's example patient into your card. Summarize the concept, not the case.
